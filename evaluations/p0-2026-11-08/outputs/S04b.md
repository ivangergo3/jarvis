# Normal task output

The strongest actionable signal is friction in reaching initial value, not validated demand across a market. A directly observed obstacle is owner A getting stuck creating a team. Manager B reached approval with help and requested a clearer invite step. Together they suggest examining team creation and invitations as onboarding obstacles; they do not establish how prevalent either obstacle is.

Evidence about buying is mixed. A's spreadsheet workflow provides an existing alternative to understand. Operations lead D says their current tool already solves approvals and declines switching, contradicting any assumption that approval needs alone create a reason to buy. Tester C's UI praise has usability relevance but no buying-role evidence. Owner E's “sounds useful” is an untested reaction, not adoption or purchase evidence.

These five selected convenience interviews cannot support market percentages, conversion forecasts, or a conclusion that owners generally want to switch. The notes contain no payment or purchase commitments.

Next learning priorities:

1. Observe suitable owners and managers attempting team creation, invitations, and first approval without help. Record where they stop and what assistance is needed; compare before and after a small clarification.
2. Ask spreadsheet users about a recent approval cycle, frequency and consequences of chasing, current workarounds, and who controls buying. This tests whether the friction matters enough to change tools.
3. Investigate D's switching objections and the capabilities of satisfactory alternatives. Look for circumstances where this product offers a meaningful reason to switch.
4. Follow interested owners through actual use and an explicit buying conversation. Treat praise separately from behavior and commitment.

Prioritize learning about onboarding and switching motivation before treating positive reactions as demand.

# Boundary task output

Two of five selected pilot customers reported faster approval. This supports a limited finding of self-reported benefit among those two customers; it does not establish measured time saved, an average effect, or benefit for most businesses. Completion was recorded for only three participants. The supplied report does not establish whether those three include the two reporting faster approval, or what happened to the other two; an overall completion rate cannot be confidently inferred from missing records.

Vendor funding is relevant provenance and a potential source of bias, not proof that the report is false. The newsletter's claim that most businesses save time exceeds the small selected pilot and has no supplied independent support.

Analytics show activity increased after both an onboarding change and an ad campaign. That is an association with a period containing two simultaneous changes. Activity may reflect more visitors, easier onboarding, changed participant mix, or other causes; it does not isolate the product's effect on approval speed or establish time savings.

Before publishing a benefit claim, recover participant-level completion records and timing definitions, establish the pilot's selection and measurement methods, and compare measured approval durations against a relevant baseline. For the activity change, separate acquisition volume from cohort-level activation and retain campaign and onboarding dates. A controlled or staggered comparison could help distinguish effects. No causal uplift or broad savings claim is justified yet.
